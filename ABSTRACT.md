# How Much Does a Warm-Up Actually Help? Per-Season ACL Risk in High-School Sports

**Introduction.** Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears can cost a teen athlete a season and often require surgery. Families weigh warm-up programs, playing surface, cleats, and knee braces, but evidence is reported as relative risks scattered across meta-analyses. None states how much a choice changes risk for athletes in a given sport–sex group. We ask: how many percentage points of seasonal ACL risk does a warm-up program move, and how robust is that estimate?

**Methods.** We combined published pooled estimates without a new systematic search. Per-season ACL risk for US high-school athletes in six sport–sex groups came from Gornitzky et al. (2016; 700 injuries). The primary warm-up effect was a meta-analytic RR of 0.50 from female team athletes; RR 0.36 (all athletes) and OR 0.28 (girls 18 and under) were alternatives. Girls' soccer and basketball were the primary groups; applying female-derived effects to boys was treated as exploratory. Published 95% CIs were propagated by Monte Carlo simulation (200,000 draws) to estimate absolute risk reduction (ARR), number needed to treat (NNT), and team-seasons per ACL prevented (assumed rosters). Results are medians with 95% uncertainty intervals (UI) that reflect uncertainty in published estimates. Sensitivity analyses assumed warm-ups prevent only non-contact tears (63% in girls, 50% in boys, all competition levels) or adherence at 75% or 50% of trial levels. Bracing and cleat evidence was reviewed non-systematically. Data, code, and sources: [https://github.com/racheepanda/teen-acl-prevention].

**Results.** Baseline seasonal risk ranged from 0.30% (boys' soccer) to 1.11% (girls' soccer). Girls' per-exposure risk was 3.7 times boys' in soccer and 3.8 times in basketball. Assuming constant risk each season, a girls' soccer player faces a 4.4% four-season risk. In the primary analysis, warm-ups lowered seasonal risk by 0.55 percentage points (95% UI 0.21–0.80) in girls' soccer and 0.44 (0.17–0.65) in girls' basketball (Table 1): NNTs of 181 and 229, or one tear prevented per about 8 and 18 team-seasons. Exploratory boys' scenarios gave smaller reductions (football 0.40, soccer 0.15). For girls' soccer, scenario medians ranged from 0.17 points (non-contact tears only, half of trial adherence; 95% UI 0.07–0.26) to 0.79 points (Figure 1). Because the model assumes one effect across sports, rankings reflect baseline differences, not sport-specific effects.

**Conclusion.** Under published effect estimates, warm-ups offer the largest absolute benefit in girls' soccer and basketball. Per athlete, the benefit is noticable (one tear prevented per roughly 180–230 girls each season) and shrinks with low adherence or if programs prevent only non-contact tears. Schools and clubs in these sports should prioritize warm-ups and track adherence. Turf evidence is contested, and neither preventive bracing nor specialty cleats has shown a reliable ACL benefit. High-school trials that track adherence and injury mechanism are the key evidence gap.
